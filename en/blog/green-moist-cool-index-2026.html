The Green-Moist-Cool Index 2026: What It Reveals About a Single Tree

NABU and the ECONICS Institute have presented the GFKI 2026 – revealing a principle that works just as well in a private garden as it does on the map of an entire country.

16 July 2026 · 4 min read · Urban Climate, Tree Cooling
← Back to Blog

Anyone interested in trees and their cooling effect has probably run into a new German metric this month: the Green-Moist-Cool Index (in German: Grün-Feucht-Kühl-Index, GFKI). The German nature conservation association NABU and the ECONICS Institute presented the latest edition in mid-July 2026 – and the findings are a good reason to shift focus from the big picture to a single tree.

What does the index actually measure?

The GFKI rates landscapes across Germany on a fine 30-by-30-metre grid, based on three factors that reinforce one another: how green an area is in late summer, how much rainfall it receives, and how cool its surface stays on hot days. The underlying idea is simple and compelling: vegetation, water and coolness aren't a side effect of healthy ecosystems – they depend on each other. Green areas evaporate water and thereby cool their surroundings; cooler, moister ground in turn lets plants thrive better.

Where this loop works, a kind of natural air conditioning emerges. Where it breaks down – through sealed surfaces, cleared agricultural land, or dying forests – heat drives further drying out, and the landscape increasingly loses its ability to regulate itself. To calculate the index, the researchers rely exclusively on measured satellite data rather than expert estimates: surface temperature, a vegetation index, and rainfall amounts are combined into a single scale from 0 to 1.

The current 2026 edition is based on the five-year period from 2021 to 2025 and, for the first time, also shows a trend: the nationwide average score has dropped significantly. Intensively farmed agricultural regions and heavily sealed cities are hit hardest, while near-natural forests, bogs and floodplains largely retain their cooling effect.

The landscape in miniature: a single tree

This is exactly where things connect to what matters here on this site. The GFKI operates at the scale of entire landscapes – but the principle it reveals works just as well on a small scale. A single mature tree is, in essence, a green-moist-cool system in miniature: its canopy provides shade, its leaves release large amounts of water through transpiration, and together these effects noticeably lower the temperature in its immediate surroundings.

A single tree is a green-moist-cool system in miniature – the same three factors the index measures across entire landscapes are also at work beneath its canopy.

Studies on urban tree cooling, as discussed in earlier posts here, regularly show temperature differences of several degrees between shaded and unshaded urban surfaces – an effect that follows exactly the same three factors the index measures.

That also explains why certain tree species get so much attention in the debate over urban greening. Fast-growing, large-leaved species such as the Paulownia evaporate an above-average amount of water per unit of leaf area, contributing relatively quickly to a local green-moist-cool effect – even though they naturally don't match the ecological depth and stability of a long-established, mixed-age, mixed-species forest.

Why this is more than an academic debate

What stands out in the current edition is that the decline isn't limited to classic heat islands like cities – it's increasingly visible in structurally poor agricultural landscapes too, regions that have so far barely featured in the public heat debate. This underlines that tree planting and urban greening, while important, can only be part of the solution. Without hedgerows, small woodlands and near-natural vegetation outside cities as well, entire landscapes risk slipping into a drying spiral that's hard to reverse.

The Heinz Lindner Tree in Karlsruhe is a tangible example of this exact principle at a small scale: a Giant Sequoia that has grown in a private garden since 1990, contributing measurably, year after year, to the green-moist-cool cycle of its surroundings through shade and transpiration.

For every single tree we plant, care for, or – as on this site – commemorate, a simple but sound idea follows: it isn't an isolated patch of green, but a small, measurable contribution to exactly the green-moist-cool cycle whose large-scale disappearance this index now documents so clearly.
