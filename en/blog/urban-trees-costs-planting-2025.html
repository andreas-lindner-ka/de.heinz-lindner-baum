Why a Single Urban Tree Can Cost Up to €40,000

German cities are missing hundreds of thousands of trees – yet even replacing them keeps failing due to cost and red tape. Two approaches show what a solution might look like.

17 July 2025 · 5 min read · Urban Climate, Heat Protection, Urban Trees
← Back to Blog

33 degrees in the shade, scorching asphalt, no greenery in sight: anyone walking through central Berlin on a summer's day quickly understands what the urban tree debate is really about. Not aesthetics – health. Trees cool their surroundings, filter the air, and absorb rainwater. And they are missing from German cities on a scale that is now having medical consequences.

Karlsruhe is among the worst-affected cities

According to the German Environment Aid (Deutsche Umwelthilfe), Karlsruhe, Mannheim, Ludwigshafen, Mainz and Frankfurt rank among the cities where heat stress hits residents hardest. Dense development, large areas of sealed surfaces and too little greenery make these cities exceptionally hot in summer. Glass facades, concrete and metal structures concentrate sunlight and radiate stored heat through the night – the urban heat island effect is especially pronounced here.

The effectiveness of trees is scientifically well established. A mature tree measurably cools its surroundings through evaporation, provides shade, and creates literally life-saving relief in overheated neighbourhoods. Disadvantaged districts are disproportionately underserved: less greenery meets more concrete and higher pollution – an inequality documented by researchers at the Technical University of Berlin in 2024.

The problem: new trees cost a fortune

That cities are making little progress despite all this evidence comes down above all to one practical obstacle: money. A new street tree today rarely costs less than €5,000 – even when planted in a spot where one previously stood. At new locations where asphalt or paving must first be removed, Munich city officials report costs of €20,000 or more. In individual cases, up to €40,000.

Why does a tree cost so much? Beneath German city streets run electricity, gas and water lines, fibre-optic cables and district heating pipes. Planting a tree requires mapping all of this, coordinating with every utility involved, and often relocating several cables or pipes. A proper planting pit by Munich's standards holds 36 cubic metres – enough room to stack two small cars on top of each other. The excavated material must be trucked away and replaced with high-quality substrate: at least four lorry journeys per tree. Add to that an administrative process that can involve multiple departments and take several weeks.
"Many people have no idea how complex the path to a single additional urban tree can be." – Jeanne-Marie Ehbauer, Head of Munich's Building Department

The effort pays off over time: trees planted in generously prepared pits with quality substrate can survive up to 60 years under favourable conditions. Their root systems develop freely, making them more resilient to exhaust fumes, road salt and drought. But in an era when cities are already failing to replace felled trees, this approach sounds like a luxury to many municipalities.

Erfurt's answer: the "fast-food tree"

In Erfurt, landscape architect Jonas Reif – professor at Erfurt University of Applied Sciences – takes a radically different approach. He plants fast-growing, low-maintenance species including a hybrid paulownia from China, autumn flame maple and fluttering elm directly into raw urban soil, with no elaborate pit preparation and no specialist substrate. His reasoning: better to have many affordable trees that develop problems after 20 years than a handful of expensive ones that barely get planted.

The Munich method

36 m³ planting pit, premium substrate, extensive utility relocation. Cost: €5,000–40,000 per tree. Lifespan: up to 60 years.

The Erfurt method

~2 m³ pit, raw soil, fast-growing exotic species. Cost: a fraction. Lifespan: ~20 years. Quick effect, easy to replace.

Reif's experimental trees in Erfurt's Poets' Quarter are holding up well despite extreme summer heat. A paulownia shot up to almost five metres in just four years – barely shorter than a neighbouring whitebeam that has been growing for around 50 years. City staff in Erfurt have taken to calling these trees "fast-food trees" internally: they do their job quickly, but are not built to last.

The Heinz-Lindner-Baum – the opposite of a fast-food tree. The giant sequoia in Karlsruhe, planted in 1990, follows neither city strategy: it stands on private land, required no administrative approval, and costs taxpayers nothing. And yet – or perhaps precisely because of this – it delivers exactly what cities are so desperately looking for: long-term cooling, CO₂ sequestration, habitat. With a potential lifespan of over 3,000 years, it outstrips any municipal planning horizon by a vast margin.

Berlin wants to act by referendum

The Berlin association BaumEntscheid has had enough of waiting for political will to materialise. It is demanding that the number of street trees in the capital be nearly doubled to close to one million by 2040 – at least one tree every 15 metres. After the Berlin Senate rejected the group's initial proposal as too expensive, BaumEntscheid is planning to force a referendum, scheduled to run alongside the Berlin state election on 20 September 2026.

Surveys cited by the association suggest that urban trees enjoy support across all political camps, from Green voters to AfD supporters alike. "There may be people who don't like children or animals," said board member Heinrich Strößenreuther, "but trees – everyone likes trees." Similar initiatives are now forming in Munich as well.

Whether cheap and fast or expensive and lasting – both approaches demonstrate that cities have the means to tackle the heat problem seriously. What is usually missing is not the technology, but the political will to act.
